You refill the 1.2-litre reservoir roughly once a month — the app warns you about a week ahead. Terra One handles the actual watering, dosing when the soil crosses your plant's moisture threshold rather than on a schedule. Refill interval depends heavily on plant size, room temperature and light; a large monstera in a warm south-facing room might need refilling every three weeks, a snake plant every two months.

The library ships with 42 profiles covering most common houseplants — monstera, pothos, snake plant, fiddle-leaf fig, peace lily, philodendrons, herbs and most succulents. You pick the species once and it loads the matching moisture band and light range. A custom profile lets you set the band yourself for anything unlisted. It is not suitable for cacti in free-draining mineral mixes, for aquatic plants, or for anything over about 1.2 m tall, where the pot becomes a tipping hazard.

No. Sensing and watering run entirely on the device using the stored profile, with no network at all. Wi-Fi only adds remote monitoring and over-the-air firmware updates. Bluetooth handles setup and nearby control. If your router dies, the planter carries on and buffers ninety days of readings to sync later.

No, and there won't be. You pay $89 once. The app is free, firmware updates are free for the life of the device, and no feature will be moved behind a paywall later. The only recurring cost is the optional nutrient cartridge, around $12 every six months, and you can run Terra One indefinitely without one.

A small regression model, trained on-device over your first two weeks, learns how fast your specific soil dries at a given temperature and humidity. That lets it project the drying curve forward and water before the plant hits stress rather than after, and lets it flag readings that deviate from the fitted model — usually a cracked pot or a root problem. There is no camera, no microphone and no large language model in the device. It cannot identify your plant by looking at it; you tell it the species once.

USB-C into any 5 V phone charger. It draws roughly 0.4 W idle and 2.5 W in the few seconds it's pumping, which works out to well under a dollar a year in electricity. There's no battery to replace. The same port takes firmware updates if you'd rather not connect it to Wi-Fi at all.

It could in earlier revisions, and it did — twice, in one pilot home, on hardware rev 2. The fix was making each dose verify itself: Terra One waits fifteen minutes after pumping, re-reads both moisture probes, and confirms the soil responded as expected. If it didn't, it stops and flags the discrepancy rather than dosing again. There's also a hard per-day volume cap in firmware. We've had no flooding reports across the pilot fleet since rev 3.
